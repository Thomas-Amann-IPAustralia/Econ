Researchers trained and tested supervised classifiers using **500 manually assessed patent triplet families**, comparing Australian patents with their European and US equivalents, then deployed the best classifier across the larger dataset.[1]

## explore-methods-3

Question: How did researchers work out whether Australian patents became narrower after the Raising the Bar reforms?

Narrative: Relevant passages describe how relative patent scope was measured (manual comparison of claims with overseas equivalents, machine learning, word counts) and the empirical strategy used to estimate the reforms' effect.

<answer label="X" coverage="complete">
<prose>
Researchers assessed **changes in Australian patent scope relative to patents for the same inventions overseas**, then modelled whether those changes were associated with the Raising the Bar (RTB) reforms.[1][4]

1. **Compare equivalent patents.** They compared Australian patents with patents in the same patent family granted by the European Patent Office and the United States Patent and Trademark Office. The study examined whether their claim scope aligned and how that alignment changed over time.[1]

2. **Classify claim scope.** They used both patent examiners’ manual assessments and a machine-learning classifier to categorise Australian claims as **narrower, similar or broader** than their overseas equivalents. They analysed the first claim, all claims, and both sets together.[2][3]

3. **Estimate the reforms’ effect.** Models estimated changes in the probability of each scope category following RTB. The reported specifications included similarity measures, an Australia–US comparison indicator and examination-section controls; Table 12 used a 2010–16 window.[3][4]

4. **Check sensitivity.** They repeated the analysis over different time windows. Estimates of the reduction in the probability of broader Australian claims were 1.0 percentage point for 2000–19, 2.0 points for 2005–19 and 4.1 points for 2010–19.[5]

**The findings supported relative narrowing, but not an unqualified causal conclusion.** The 2024 Australian IP Report summarised the estimated reduction in broader patents as 1.0–4.0 percentage points; the detailed research report reported an effect as high as 5.7 points. The IP Report cautioned that narrowing had begun before the reforms, although results were robust to focusing on the period immediately before and after them.[6][7] Relative changes could also partly reflect changes in US or European patent scope, rather than Australian changes alone.[8]

The research points towards **helping small businesses use IP early and strategically, rather than simply encouraging more registrations**.[1][3]

- **Embed IP advice early in business development.** The 2022 report recommends ensuring small businesses have the information needed to make informed protection decisions.[1] Supporting early engagement is consistent with Australian evidence: SMEs filing for IP were 16 per cent more likely to achieve high employment growth, and startups filing in their first year were twice as likely. These are estimated associations, not proof that filing causes growth.[2]
- **Prioritise quality and commercial use over the number of rights.** The profitability research explicitly suggests focusing policy on the quality of rights, the innovations protected and how businesses exploit them. Although IP ownership was associated with higher profitability, the number of rights held was not significantly associated with profitability.[3]
- **Preserve flexibility and help firms use it strategically.** Research reported in 2025 found that flexibility over patent application timeframes benefits small innovators engaging early, through more innovations, more commercialised products and greater lifetime IP value.[4]
- **Support different commercialisation pathways.** Small firms may need time to secure finance and partners, and to choose between commercialising an invention themselves or licensing or selling it to a larger firm. Early IP engagement can help them assess these alternatives.[5]
- **Provide practical application support.** The 2022 report describes dedicated SME case management, eligibility for faster patent examination, and accessible education, tools and checklists as ways to help SMEs navigate protection.[6]
- **Extend support to disputes and enforcement.** The 2026 report describes IP First Response as a pilot offering early, structured guidance for both rights holders facing suspected infringement and businesses accused of infringement, including information on pathways, costs, risks and outcomes.[7]
