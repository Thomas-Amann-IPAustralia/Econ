How it works

This assistant answers questions from one source only: the publications of IP Australia's Office of the Chief Economist. That is 24 documents and 1,099 pages: the Australian IP Reports from 2015 to 2026, and 12 economic research papers and notes from 2014 to 2025. It does not use the internet or general knowledge for facts and figures. Every statement it makes from those publications points to the sentences it rests on, and every sentence leads to its page in the original PDF.

What happens when you ask a question

  1. Understand the question. A small AI model rewrites a follow-up question so it makes sense on its own, and breaks a broad question into up to four separate searches. You can see the searches it chose in the evidence trail.
  2. Search the publications. Each search looks for passages two ways: by meaning, and by the exact words used. The two result lists are combined, and the best 60 passages go forward, with no more than eight from any one document so that one report can't crowd out the rest.
  3. Choose the best evidence. A small AI model reads all 60 passages and rates how useful each would be for answering your question. The 20 rated highest become the evidence. It only chooses passages and never changes their text. The evidence trail lists all 60 with their ratings, so you can see what was left out.
  4. Write the answer. A larger AI model writes the answer from those 20 passages and nothing else. It doesn't copy quotes itself. Instead it points to the numbered sentences each statement rests on, and the system copies those sentences, word for word, from the publication.
  5. Check the answer. Ordinary code, not an AI model, then checks that every cited sentence comes from a passage the model was actually given, and that every claim in the answer is backed by at least one. If the answer fails, the model gets one more try. An answer that still fails is shown with a warning.

How well it has been tested

Every part of the system was measured against a set of 178 test questions before it was put in place. Some ask for a specific fact ("What share of Australian businesses own a registered IP right, and how has that changed?"). Others are broad, exploratory questions of the kind staff ask when researching a topic. Five of them are real questions from IP Australia staff. Half the questions were used to make design choices, and the other half were kept back to confirm them. For each question, an AI grader rated thousands of passages for whether they helped answer it. A second AI, from a different company, independently re-graded a sample, and the two agreed on 95% of those passages.

It was also given 18 awkward questions: off-topic requests, questions about current events, questions built on a false premise, and pasted text pretending to be instructions. It declined the off-topic ones, gave the year of any figure it found for current events, corrected both false premises, and ignored the instructions hidden in the pasted text. Two weaknesses showed up. It sometimes labels an answer "Complete" when "Partial" would be fairer. And it can be talked into tasks that have nothing to do with the publications, such as writing a poem.

Reading an answer

  • Blue text ending in a number rests on the sentences that number cites. Select the number and the evidence trail goes to those sentences, highlighted in their passage.
  • Text in the ordinary colour, with no number, is the assistant's own words: an explanation of a term, a connection between findings, or a judgement. No publication is cited for it. Bold, headings and lists are the assistant's formatting, to help you read.
  • Claims made by the chatbot, the second tab on each answer, lists every claim in the assistant's words above the sentences it cites, copied word for word. A claim drawn from more than one passage shows its sentences in green, there and in the evidence trail. Step through the claims with Previous and Next, and the evidence trail follows, passage by passage.
  • Download the conversation (or copy it) as a JSON file to look further into the results: every question, the searches, the filter's rating of every passage it read, the answer, its claims and their quotes, the check, the timings and the cost. Passages are listed by their identifiers and page links, without their full text.

What you can rely on, and what to check yourself

You can rely on these:

  • Every highlighted quote is copied exactly from a publication, by code. The AI model can't change a quote's wording, and it can't cite a passage it wasn't shown.
  • Every quote links to its page in the original report, so you can read it in context.
  • Every passage shows the year it was published.

Check these yourself:

  • Whether a quote supports the claim it's attached to. No check covers this yet. Blue text shows that a stretch of the answer cites sentences, not that they bear out every word of it: the assistant writes in its own words and can add its own connections. Read the cited sentences, especially for any point your work depends on.
  • How the findings are joined up. When the answer connects findings ("Taken together…"), that is the AI's reading of the evidence, not a finding from the reports.
  • Explanations of general terms, such as what a regression is, may be given without a citation. They are there to help you follow the answer and add no facts about IP, firms or the economy.
  • How current a figure is. The publications run from 2014 to 2026, and a figure is only as recent as the report it comes from. The assistant knows nothing published since.
  • The 2018 and 2021 reports were scanned, so their text was read from page images. 62 passages came out garbled. 55 of them were re-read and repaired, and the other 7 turned out to be correct as printed. If a quote from these reports looks odd, the PDF page is the authority.
  • "Complete" and "Partial" are the AI's own judgement of how fully the publications answer your question. Treat them as a guide, not a guarantee.

Limits of the testing

Most test questions were written for testing, from the reports themselves. Real questions will vary, and the five real staff questions are the closest the tests come to everyday use. The graders and judges are AI models. They were checked against each other but not against IP Australia's economists. The side-by-side comparison of answers used an earlier version of the answer-writing instructions. The current version, which cites sentences, was checked on six questions, and all six passed the citation check first time.

When no AI model is available

The service switches to evidence-only mode. It shows the most relevant passages with their sources, but writes no answer.

The documents and passages shown here are from IP Australia's public reports, as published at ipaustralia.gov.au. The published reports are the authoritative source.
