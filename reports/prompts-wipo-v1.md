### Why should year-to-year swings in plant breeder's rights numbers be read with caution?

**before** (complete, 16 claims, passed, 28.1 s)

### Small numbers can produce big percentage changes
PBR application volumes are modest compared with patents and trade marks, so relatively small changes in numbers can produce large percentage movements. [2] Filing decisions by a small number of applicants or large breeding programs can therefore strongly influence the annual result, even when underlying breeding investment remains steady. [3]

The latest figures illustrate why it helps to look beneath the total: in 2025, Australian resident applications rose by 17.0%, from 100 to 117, while non-resident applications fell by 19.2%, from 182 to 147. [4] Overall applications fell by 6.4%, from 282 to 264—the third consecutive annual decline. [5] The 2026 report cautions that the narrowing gap between resident and non-resident filings likely reflects volatility rather than a structural change in international engagement. [6]

### Registrations are not a measure of the same year’s applications
Registering a PBR typically takes around 2.5 years, including substantive examination and a comparative growing trial. [7] There is consequently little correlation between applications filed in a year and registrations completed that year. [8]

Registration totals can also move sharply because of operational timing:
- **Staffing:** registrations fell by 51% to 111 in 2016, with the fall attributed to fewer staff able to register PBRs; registrations then more than doubled to 245 in 2017 as they recovered from that trough. [9]
- **Growing trials and processing:** pandemic travel restrictions made growing trials harder to organise and attend, while the resumption of field examinations contributed to higher registrations in 2023. [10] Lower registrations in 2024 were attributed to delays in journal publication and registration processing. [11]
- **The latest rebound:** the 2026 report records registrations rising from 124 in 2024 to 169 in 2025, attributing this to examination timing and applications moving through the process rather than a structural shift in filing behaviour. [12] The earlier 2025 report recorded 127 registrations for 2024 rather than 124; the supplied passages do not explain that difference. [13]

### Caution does not mean ignoring longer-term changes
The reports also identify changes extending beyond a single year: average annual applications fell from 369 in 2015–2018 to 299 in 2019–2022, a decline of 19.0%. [14] They suggest that economic losses from the 2019–20 bushfires and the 2017–2019 droughts likely contributed to declining resident filings. [15]

**Taken together, the evidence suggests reading annual totals alongside the longer-term trend, who is filing, and what is happening in the examination process—rather than treating a single rise or fall as a direct measure of breeding investment.** [16]

### How does the Global Innovation Index decide what counts as an economy's strength?

**before** (complete, 9 claims, passed, 22.4 s)

The **Global Innovation Index (GII) identifies strengths mainly as an economy’s best-performing indicators—not necessarily areas where it leads the world**. It also automatically treats a world ranking of first, second or third as a strength, subject to data-coverage requirements. [1]

### How the main strength label works
For the **2026 GII**, the process is:

1. **Convert indicator scores into percentile ranks.** This puts performance across different indicators onto a comparable relative scale. [2]
2. **Find the economy’s strongest indicators.** Among its 79 indicators, the 10th-highest percentile rank sets the threshold; indicators at or above that threshold count as strengths. Ties can therefore produce more than 10 strengths. [3]
3. **Include any top-three world rankings.** An indicator ranked first, second or third automatically qualifies, regardless of the percentile threshold. [4]

So the main label is best read as **a standout within that economy’s own innovation profile**, rather than a requirement to clear one fixed worldwide performance threshold. [5]

### A separate label compares performance with peers
The GII also identifies **income-group strengths**: these are scores above the group average by more than one standard deviation—a measure of how spread out scores are. For the top 25 high-income economies, the comparison is with that top-25 group instead. [6]

The profile symbols distinguish the two: a **solid circle** marks a main strength, while a **solid diamond** marks an income-group strength. [7]

### Data safeguards matter
The indicator-based threshold is also applied to broader pillars and sub-pillars, but no strength or weakness is flagged for those that fail the minimum data-coverage requirement. [8] The percentile-based labels also require sufficiently recent data: no older than five years before the indicator’s most common reference year—for example, 2019 or later where that reference year is 2024. [9]

**after** (complete, 11 claims, passed, 27.1 s)

In WIPO’s **Global Innovation Index (GII) 2026**, a strength is generally an area where an economy performs especially well compared with its performance across the other indicators—not simply an area where it scores above average. ### How the main strength label works
1. **Put indicators on a comparable footing.** WIPO converts each indicator score into a percentile rank—a way of expressing its relative position. [2]
2. **Identify the economy’s strongest indicators.** Among its 79 indicators, WIPO uses the tenth-highest percentile rank as the cut-off for strengths; ties can mean more than ten indicators qualify. [3]
3. **Include top-three world rankings.** An indicator ranked first, second or third automatically counts as a strength, regardless of that percentile cut-off. [4]

The important distinction is that this identifies the strongest parts of an economy’s own innovation profile; WIPO has a separate label for strength relative to an income-group benchmark. [5]

### What about pillars and data quality?
The same indicator-based cut-off is also applied to **pillars and sub-pillars**, the broader categories that group indicators together. [6] However, WIPO does not flag strengths or weaknesses for pillars or sub-pillars that fail its minimum data-coverage requirements; these are shown in brackets in the economy profiles. [7]

There is also a data-age rule for the percentile-based labels: observations must be no more than five years older than the indicator’s most common data year—for example, 2019 or later when that year is 2024. [8]

### A separate label: strength against peers
An **income-group strength** means the economy’s score is more than one standard deviation above its income-group average.
